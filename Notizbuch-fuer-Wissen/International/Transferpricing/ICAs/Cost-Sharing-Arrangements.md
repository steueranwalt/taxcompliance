It’s the fourth in a series of 6 workshops for 6 teams of TP professionals who have signed up for the program - something we launched earlier this year, and will extend in 2025.

  


CSAs put in place by Microsoft and AirBnB have featured prominently in the tax news lately, and both those MNEs are in dispute with the IRS regarding eye-watering amounts (US$ 28.9 billion in the case of Microsoft, and US$ 1.35 billion in the case of AirBnB).

  


Interestingly, at LCN we’re still seeing a relatively consistent appetite amongst MNEs to enter into CSAs. Obviously it’s important to get the legal aspects of any TP arrangement right - but it’s even more critical for CSAs. This is due to 3 main factors:

  


1\. The highly prescriptive provisions of the US Regs, which not only set out a list of mandatory provisions to be included in the implementing agreement, but also specifies a hard deadline for signing and dating the agreement (60 days after inception);

  


2\. The role of platform contribution transactions, which usually need to be documented separately, and which may occur on inception, during the life of the CSA, and on termination (or partial exit); and

  


3\. The critical role of IP and intangibles, and the necessity to establish clearly what legal rights in intangibles are involved, which entities currently hold them, and which entities should hold them on termination of the arrangement.

  


This last point - where IP should end up - is often overlooked in the planning of CSAs (and profit splits), but is actually fundamental to the economic analysis. This is because the ongoing ownership of IP (both legacy IP and newly created IP) is one aspect of allocation of reward, which is just as significant economically as the allocation of risk.

  


This is why projects to plan, design and implement a CSA often need to be treated as mini group reorganisation, with an appropriate level of legal due diligence and cross-functional input.
